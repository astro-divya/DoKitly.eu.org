What a derivative can and cannot tell you

The derivative describes the instantaneous rate at which a function changes as its input changes. For f(x) = x³, the symbolic derivative is 3x². At x = 2, the slope is 12 units of output per unit of input; that is not the function value, which is 8. A practical example is a distance model s(t) = 5t² measured in metres: its derivative 10t gives velocity in metres per second when t is in seconds. Units help catch confusion between an original quantity and its rate of change.

When entering a function, use the syntax supported by the expression parser, especially around multiplication, exponents and parentheses. If the field expects a variable name, choose the symbol that actually varies. Differentiating x²y with respect to x while treating y as constant differs from differentiating it with respect to y. Higher-order derivatives mean differentiating the previous derivative again: for f(x) = x³, first order is 3x², second order is 6x and third order is 6. The selected order should match the question being asked.

Differentiability, corners, and domain boundaries

Not every function has a derivative at every point. The absolute-value function |x| has a sharp corner at zero; its left-hand slope is −1 and right-hand slope is +1, so there is no single derivative there. A function can also become undefined outside its natural domain: ln(x) over real numbers requires x greater than zero. If a symbolic tool produces a formula with a denominator, inspect points at which that denominator becomes zero. A mathematical expression may be valid over most values and invalid at one important boundary.

As a sanity check, estimate the slope numerically: calculate [f(x+h) − f(x−h)]/(2h) using a small nonzero h and compare with the symbolic derivative at an interior point. Do not use an extremely tiny h in floating-point arithmetic because subtraction can amplify rounding error. For f(x)=x² at x=3, the centred estimate should be near 6. This numerical technique is a cross-check, not a replacement for exact differentiation and domain analysis.

Typical reading mistakes

A derivative equal to zero identifies a stationary point, not automatically a maximum. f(x)=x³ has zero derivative at x=0 but no local maximum there. Likewise, a positive derivative over an interval suggests increasing behaviour there, but that statement must be applied only where the function is defined and differentiable. If the tool displays notation such as d/dx or f'(x), read it as an operation on a function. Keep the original formula in your notes so results are not mistaken for equations to solve.

A second way to read a slope

Consider a business model whose revenue in rupees is R(q)=100q−q² for selling q units in a simplified setting. The derivative R'(q)=100−2q expresses marginal revenue. At q=20 it equals 60, meaning a small increase in quantity near that point changes revenue at an approximate rate of sixty rupees per additional unit. This is not the same as the average revenue R(q)/q. The model itself is an illustration: practical pricing decisions require market data, integer quantities and costs not represented in this one mathematical function. Reading the output with its units prevents a correct differentiation from becoming an unsupported business conclusion.

Stationary points that are not maxima or minima

A graph of y = x³ has a horizontal tangent at x = 0 because its derivative 3x² becomes zero there, but that does not make x = 0 a local maximum or minimum. This example is a useful check against assuming every stationary point is a turning point. Choose the expression and differentiation variable on the dedicated page, and decide whether the answer should be a symbolic derivative or a numerical slope at a particular value. An output containing x is a derivative function, not necessarily a single rate. Before copying into homework, evaluate an easy input and compare against a small finite-difference estimate where the function is smooth.

Common questions about Differentiation Calculator

How can I check a polynomial derivative?

Differentiate each term separately and compare signs and powers; a constant contributes zero.

Does a derivative always describe speed?

Only when the original function represents position as a function of time. For other functions, it measures change in the relevant units.

Does a zero derivative always mean a peak or trough?

No. For x³ at zero, the derivative vanishes even though the curve keeps increasing. Additional analysis is necessary.

Why might a derivative be undefined at a corner?

A corner can have different left and right slopes. A single tangent slope does not exist unless those limits agree.

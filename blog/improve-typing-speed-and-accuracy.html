How to Improve Typing Speed and Accuracy

Improve WPM with a practical typing routine that prioritizes accuracy, touch typing, posture, mistake analysis and consistent progress measurement.

Practical guideUpdated for DoKitly Build 21

Speed comes from efficient habits

Typing faster is not mainly about moving your fingers harder. It comes from reducing hesitation, building reliable key memory and avoiding corrections. A typist who races at high speed but constantly backspaces can produce less useful work than someone slightly slower with excellent accuracy.

The best training therefore combines speed and accuracy rather than treating them as separate goals.

Understand WPM and accuracy

Words per minute, or WPM, is a standardized way to estimate typing speed. Different tests can calculate it slightly differently, so compare results from the same test setup when tracking progress. Accuracy measures how much of your input matches the target text.

A high WPM result with poor accuracy is not automatically better. In real writing, errors cost time because they must be noticed and corrected.

Learn touch typing

Touch typing means finding keys primarily through finger memory instead of looking down repeatedly. The goal is not to memorize the keyboard as a picture; it is to develop a physical map. Start with consistent finger positions, then practice transitions between keys until common patterns feel automatic.

If you currently type with two or three fingers, changing technique can temporarily make you slower. That is normal. Build the new movement pattern carefully before measuring maximum speed.

Posture and keyboard position

Sit comfortably with relaxed shoulders. Keep wrists neutral rather than sharply bent upward, and place the keyboard at a height that does not force your elbows or shoulders into tension. You do not need a rigid “perfect” pose, but discomfort and excessive force make long sessions harder.

Use light key presses. Most modern keyboards do not need heavy impact, and extra force can slow transitions between keys.

A practical 20-minute routine

  1. Five minutes of accuracy work. Type slowly enough to stay controlled. Focus on problem letters and transitions.
  2. Five minutes of common words. Practice frequently used patterns so whole chunks become familiar.
  3. Five minutes of timed typing. Use the DoKitly Typing Test or another consistent test to measure WPM and errors.
  4. Five minutes of review. Identify repeated mistakes instead of immediately starting another speed run.

Short daily sessions are usually more sustainable than one exhausting practice session each week.

Accuracy before maximum speed

When you repeatedly type a key sequence incorrectly, you are practicing the wrong movement. Slow down enough to produce the correct pattern. Once accuracy becomes reliable, gradually increase pace.

A useful technique is to practice just below your maximum speed, where you still feel fluent and controlled. Occasionally test your limit, but spend most training time reinforcing clean input.

Use different difficulty levels

Easy passages help build rhythm. Medium passages introduce longer words and varied punctuation. Hard material can expose weaknesses in numbers, capitalization, symbols or unusual vocabulary. Rotate difficulty instead of practicing only the text style that gives you the highest score.

Learn from errors

Keep a small list of recurring problem patterns. Maybe you confuse I and O, mistype “tion,” struggle with capital letters, or lose rhythm after punctuation. Build mini drills around those problems. Ten focused repetitions can be more valuable than another generic paragraph.

Backspace and strict modes

Different typing tests use different rules. Some allow corrections, some lock completed words, and some disable backspace. These modes measure different skills. A no-backspace mode can train commitment and accuracy, while a normal mode better reflects everyday correction behavior. Compare scores only when the rules are similar.

Do not stare at the live WPM number

Watching the speed counter every few seconds can break rhythm. Focus on the text and let the result appear at the end. If the test shows live statistics, treat them as background information rather than a target you must chase with every word.

Measure progress consistently

Use the same duration and similar difficulty when comparing results. Record WPM, accuracy and error count. A meaningful improvement might be a modest WPM increase with fewer errors, not only a new personal speed record.

Progress is rarely linear. Fatigue, keyboard changes, unfamiliar text and concentration can all change a single result. Look at averages across several sessions.

Common mistakes that slow progress

Practicing only at maximum speed

This can reinforce errors and tension. Mix controlled practice with occasional speed tests.

Looking at the keyboard after every mistake

Try to correct from finger memory when possible. Frequent visual checking interrupts the mental map you are trying to build.

Ignoring punctuation and capitals

Real-world typing includes more than lowercase words. Include realistic text as your fundamentals improve.

Changing tests constantly

Different scoring systems can make progress look bigger or smaller than it really is. Keep one consistent benchmark.

Practice difficult transitions, not only difficult keys

Many typing errors come from the movement between two keys rather than from forgetting one key. You may know where both letters are but still stumble on combinations such as “tion,” “ing,” repeated letters or a capital followed by punctuation. Identify these transitions and practice them as short chunks.

Chunk practice also helps common words become single motor patterns. Instead of consciously selecting every letter in “because” or “different,” your fingers gradually learn the sequence as a unit.

Use real writing as part of training

Typing tests are useful because they create consistent measurements, but everyday writing adds planning, punctuation and correction decisions. Mix test practice with emails, notes or transcription so your skill transfers beyond a score screen. When doing real writing, do not force maximum speed; maintain a comfortable rhythm and clean output.

Protect your hands

More practice is not useful if it creates pain. Take breaks, reduce force and adjust your workspace if you notice persistent discomfort, numbness or strain. Typing improvement should come from coordination and familiarity, not from pushing through pain.

A balanced practice session

A practical session can be short and still useful. Begin with a few minutes of slow accuracy work, then complete one timed test at a comfortable pace, followed by a focused drill on the letters or word patterns that caused the most errors. Finish with another timed run without forcing speed. Record both WPM and accuracy so improvement is not measured by speed alone. If accuracy falls sharply, reduce pace and rebuild rhythm. Consistent practice with relaxed hands and correct finger movement is usually more useful than repeatedly chasing a personal-best score.

FAQ

How fast can I improve my WPM?

There is no guaranteed rate. Progress depends on your starting technique, practice quality, consistency and the difficulty of the text.

Should I prioritize speed or accuracy?

Build accuracy first, then increase speed while trying to preserve it. Fast error-filled typing creates extra correction work.

Is a one-minute test enough?

It is useful for a quick benchmark, but longer tests reveal whether you can maintain speed and accuracy over time.

Should I practice every day?

Frequent short sessions can help build motor memory, but rest when your hands feel tired or uncomfortable.

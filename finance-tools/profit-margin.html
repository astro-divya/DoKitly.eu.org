Distinguishing gross margin from markup

Profit margin is profit divided by SELLING price; markup is profit divided by COST. If an item costs ₹80 and sells for ₹100, gross profit is ₹20. The gross margin is 20/100 = 20%, while the markup is 20/80 = 25%. Both are correct but describe different bases. A shop setting prices with a desired 25% margin must not simply add 25% to cost: 25% markup on ₹80 produces ₹100, whose margin is only 20%.

To achieve a target gross margin of 25% on a cost of ₹80, selling price must be cost divided by 1−0.25, or about ₹106.67 before relevant taxes and fees. In contrast, a target 25% markup gives ₹100. Use the correct target and confirm whether the UI is asking for cost, selling price or profit percentage. Negative gross profit is possible: buying at ₹120 and selling at ₹100 means a ₹20 loss and −20% margin calculated on selling price.

Gross profit is not the full business result

Cost of goods sold may cover purchase cost and directly attributable costs, but business net profit can also depend on rent, salaries, payment charges, refunds, advertising and financing. If you calculate margin using only the invoice purchase price, you have not necessarily measured the profitability of the entire transaction. For a seller with ₹100 selling price, ₹70 purchase cost and ₹15 marketplace/shipping fees, the contribution after those stated costs is ₹15, not ₹30. A margin tool cannot infer unentered expenses.

Percentage targets can become misleading when sale prices vary. A 40% gross margin on a ₹50 item contributes ₹20, while a 20% margin on a ₹500 item contributes ₹100. For stock and promotions, compare absolute rupees, volume, returns and inventory turnover rather than ranking products on percentage alone. Calculate each product using the same assumptions before aggregating a weighted overall margin.

Rechecking a pricing calculation

A reliable manual check is to subtract cost from sale price and then divide by the stated denominator. For a ₹250 sale and ₹175 cost, profit is ₹75; margin is 75/250=30%, and markup is 75/175≈42.86%. If the screen returns values near these, the underlying formula likely matches the selected label. Rounding target sale prices down to the nearest rupee can slightly reduce the achieved margin, so inspect the final price rather than only the desired percentage.

Discounts change gross margin nonlinearly

A product costing ₹600 and normally selling at ₹1,000 yields ₹400 gross profit and a 40% margin. A 20% promotional discount makes the price ₹800, but leaves cost unchanged at ₹600; gross profit falls to ₹200 and margin falls to 25%. The discount cut the selling price by 20% while halving gross profit. This is why promotion planning should compare contribution rupees and margin, not just the advertised percentage off the original retail price.

Markup, gross margin and selling price calculations

Buying an item for ₹80 and selling it for ₹100 leaves ₹20 gross profit. Markup is ₹20/₹80 = 25%, while gross margin is ₹20/₹100 = 20%. The reference denominator changes the percentage even though the cash profit is identical. A business may still lose money after rent, shipping, payment fees and returns, so gross margin is not the same as net profit. When pricing a product with discounts, compute the final selling price before recalculating the margin. If you want a target 25% gross margin on an ₹80 cost, price at roughly ₹106.67, not ₹100.

In a marketplace example, a seller buys an item for ₹500 and lists it for ₹750. Gross profit before fees is ₹250, but a ₹75 marketplace fee and ₹45 packaging cost leave ₹130 before any further expenses. A headline gross margin computed only from purchase cost does not describe take-home income. Define which costs belong in cost of goods sold, and keep consistent assumptions when comparing product lines.

COMMON QUESTIONS, SIMPLE ANSWERS

Common questions about Profit Margin Calculator

Why is markup larger than margin for the same sale?

The markup divides profit by cost, while margin divides it by the selling price.

Can a product have a positive margin but lose money overall?

Yes. Gross margin may exclude shipping, rent, advertising and other expenses needed to sell the item.

How do I calculate a selling price from target margin?

Divide the cost by one minus the desired margin expressed as a decimal, before other costs and taxes.

Can positive gross margin coexist with negative net profit?

Yes. Operating expenses and other costs can exceed the gross profit left after direct product cost.
